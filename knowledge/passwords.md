# Passwords

## What makes a strong password, and how long should it be?
Length matters most. A long, unique password is far harder to guess than a short complicated one. Aim for at least 12 characters and ideally 16 or more. The best choice is a passphrase of four or more random, unrelated words, such as "river lantern copper walnut", because it is both strong and easier to remember than a jumble of symbols. Avoid names, birthdays, favourite teams, keyboard patterns and anything that appears on social media.

## Why should every account have a different password?
Attackers take passwords leaked from one breached site and try them on email, social media, banking and school accounts. This is called credential stuffing and it works because many people reuse passwords. If every account has its own password, one breach stays one breach. Your email account matters most of all: it can reset passwords everywhere else, so give it a strong, unique password and multi-factor authentication.

## What is a password manager and should I use one?
A password manager stores a different strong password for every account behind one master password. It also fills passwords only on the correct website, which helps defeat fake login pages. You need to remember just one long master passphrase, which should be unique and never reused. Turn on multi-factor authentication for the manager itself and keep recovery information somewhere safe. Using a reputable manager is much safer than reusing passwords or storing them in notes, spreadsheets or photos.

## What are passkeys?
A passkey replaces a password with a cryptographic key pair stored on your device and unlocked with your fingerprint, face or device PIN. Because there is no password to type, there is nothing to phish or reuse, and a passkey only works on the real website. Passkeys are supported by more services every year. Where offered, they are a stronger and easier option than passwords.

## Modern password guidance
Current guidance, such as NIST's, recommends allowing long passwords, checking new passwords against lists of leaked and common passwords, and not forcing frequent changes without a reason. Change a password when there is evidence it has been exposed, not on a fixed schedule. Composition rules (one capital, one symbol) matter less than length and uniqueness.

## How do attackers crack passwords?
- **Guessing:** trying common passwords like "123456" and "password".
- **Dictionary and pattern attacks:** words with predictable changes ("P@ssw0rd!").
- **Brute force:** trying every combination, which becomes impractical as length grows.
- **Credential stuffing:** reusing leaked passwords across sites.
- **Phishing and keyloggers:** simply stealing the password instead of guessing it.
Well-run websites store only salted, hashed passwords, so a leak doesn't reveal them directly, but weak passwords can still be cracked from hashes.

## How do I know if my password has been leaked?
Check your email address on the Have I Been Pwned website, which lists known data breaches. If an account appears in a breach, change that password and any other account that used it, and turn on multi-factor authentication. Many browsers and password managers also warn you about reused or leaked passwords.

## Security questions and password safety habits
Treat answers to security questions like passwords, because real answers (a pet's name, your school) are often easy to find. Where a site forces them, use a random answer stored in your password manager. Never share your password, even with friends or someone claiming to be IT staff. Don't save passwords in plain text files or send them in messages. Use the Password Security page on this site to test a password, since it runs only in your browser.
