# Incident Response

## What is a security incident?
A security incident is any event that harms or threatens the confidentiality, integrity or availability of data or systems. For a student, that could be a hacked account, a phishing click, malware on a laptop, a lost or stolen device, leaked personal data or a scam that cost money. Reporting early matters more than being sure: it is better to report a false alarm than to hide a real problem.

## What are the stages of incident response?
Professional frameworks such as NIST's describe four stages: **Preparation** (backups, updates, knowing who to contact), **Detection and analysis** (noticing and understanding what happened), **Containment, eradication and recovery** (stopping the damage, removing the cause, restoring normal service) and **Post-incident learning** (finding what failed and improving). Everyday incidents follow the same pattern on a smaller scale.

## What should I do first when something goes wrong?
1. Stay calm and don't delete anything.
2. Stop further harm: disconnect from the network, sign out of the account, or lock the device.
3. Write down what you noticed and when.
4. Change affected passwords from a different, clean device.
5. Tell your school's IT or security contact.
6. File an incident report on this site.
7. Follow IT's instructions before restoring or reinstalling anything.

## What if my account was hacked?
Try to log in and change the password. If you can't, use the service's account-recovery process. Then enable multi-factor authentication, sign out all other sessions and devices, and check recovery email, recovery phone, forwarding rules and connected apps for changes you didn't make. Change the password on any other account that used the same one. Warn your contacts that messages from you may be fake, and report the incident.

## What if my device is lost or stolen?
Use Find My Device or Find My iPhone to locate, lock or erase it. Change passwords for accounts that were signed in on it and sign those sessions out. Contact your mobile carrier to block the SIM if needed, and inform your school and, for theft, the police. Full-disk encryption and a strong screen lock greatly reduce the risk to your data.

## What evidence should I keep?
Take screenshots of suspicious messages, error messages, ransom notes, unfamiliar login alerts and anything unusual. Keep the original email rather than only forwarding it, because the headers help investigators. Note the date, time, device, account and what you did. Don't clean up before reporting, so important clues aren't lost.

## How do I choose the severity of a report?
- **Low:** an attempt that failed or caused no harm, such as a phishing email you deleted without clicking.
- **Medium:** a possible exposure or minor impact, such as clicking a link without entering information.
- **High:** a confirmed compromise, such as a hacked account, malware infection or credentials entered on a fake page.
- **Critical:** serious, widespread or ongoing harm, such as ransomware, financial loss or exposure of sensitive personal data.
If unsure, choose the higher level; the school can adjust it.

## What makes a good incident report?
State the type of incident, what happened in order, the date and time, the affected device or account, any suspected cause, the actions you've already taken and anything you're unsure about. Be factual rather than blaming. The report form on this site asks for exactly these details, and the more precise you are, the faster the response.
