# Ransomware

## What is ransomware?
Ransomware is malware that encrypts your files, or locks you out of your device, and demands payment for the key. The files are still there, but unreadable without the attacker's decryption key. Victims range from home users and schools to hospitals and large companies.

## How ransomware works
It usually arrives through a phishing email, a malicious download or an unpatched vulnerability. Once running, it quietly searches for files, encrypts documents, photos, databases and sometimes network drives and backups, then shows a ransom note with a deadline and a payment demand, often in cryptocurrency. Some strains spread across a network before encrypting, to cause maximum damage.

## What is double extortion?
Modern attackers often steal a copy of the data before encrypting it. They then threaten to publish it if the ransom isn't paid, so even a victim who restores from backups still faces a data leak. This is why backups alone don't fully solve a ransomware incident, and why quick reporting matters.

## How ransomware spreads
- Phishing emails with malicious attachments or links.
- Fake software, cracked games and pirated tools.
- Remote desktop or other services exposed to the internet with weak passwords.
- Unpatched software and operating systems.
- Stolen credentials bought from criminal markets.
- Ransomware-as-a-service, where criminals rent ready-made ransomware, so even unskilled attackers can use it.

## How to protect yourself from ransomware
1. Back up important files using the 3-2-1 rule: 3 copies, on 2 different types of storage, with 1 kept offline or off-site.
2. Test restoring from your backup occasionally; an untested backup may not work.
3. Install updates promptly.
4. Use multi-factor authentication on all important accounts.
5. Avoid pirated software and unexpected attachments.
6. Use a standard user account for everyday work.
7. Keep security software enabled.

## What should I do if my files are encrypted or I see a ransom note?
Disconnect the device from Wi-Fi and the network immediately so it can't spread or reach shared drives. Don't delete anything; take a photo of the ransom note. Tell your school's IT or security contact straight away and file an incident report. Don't use the infected device to log in anywhere, and change passwords from a clean device. Don't start restoring from a backup until IT confirms the infection is removed, or it may be encrypted again.

## Should the ransom be paid?
Law enforcement agencies generally advise against paying. Payment doesn't guarantee you get working files back, it marks you as a willing payer, and it funds further crime. Some victims who paid were attacked again. Before anything else, report the incident and check whether a free decryptor exists at the No More Ransom project (nomoreransom.org), which collects decryptors for many ransomware families.

## Warning signs of an attack in progress
Files suddenly renamed with strange extensions, documents that won't open, a ransom note on screen or in folders, sudden heavy disk activity, or security software being switched off. If you see these, disconnecting from the network quickly can limit the damage.
