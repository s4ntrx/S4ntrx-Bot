# Social Engineering

## What is social engineering?
Social engineering is manipulating people, rather than breaking technology, to get information, access or money. Attackers exploit trust, helpfulness, fear and curiosity. Phishing is the best-known form, but the same tricks appear in phone calls, text messages, social media and face-to-face visits. A strong firewall can't help if someone is persuaded to open the door.

## Why does social engineering work?
Attackers use well-known psychological levers: **authority** (pretending to be a teacher, boss or police), **urgency and scarcity** ("act now or lose access"), **fear**, **reciprocity** (giving a small favour first), **liking and familiarity** (acting friendly or mentioning people you know), **social proof** ("everyone else already did it"), and **curiosity** (a tempting file or message). They are designed to make you act before you think.

## Common social engineering attacks
- **Pretexting:** inventing a believable story or identity, such as "I'm from IT and need to verify your account".
- **Baiting:** leaving an infected USB drive or offering a "free" download.
- **Quid pro quo:** offering help or a reward in exchange for information, like fake tech support.
- **Tailgating or piggybacking:** following an authorised person through a secure door.
- **Shoulder surfing:** watching you enter a PIN or password.
- **Impersonation:** posing as a friend, relative or official on social media or the phone.
- **Vishing and smishing:** phone and text-message scams.

## What are AI voice cloning and deepfake scams?
Attackers can now copy a person's voice from a short clip or create fake video. A common scam is a call or voice note that sounds like a family member or boss urgently asking for money or codes. Slow down and hang up. Call the person back on a number you already have, ask a question only they would know, and agree on a family code word in advance. Don't trust a voice, face or caller ID alone.

## What are typical scam scenarios?
- A "bank" or "mobile-money" message saying your account is frozen and asking for a PIN or one-time code.
- "I sent money to you by mistake, please send it back."
- A job or scholarship offer that asks you to pay a fee or share ID documents.
- A "friend" messaging from a new account asking for money or a verification code.
- A caller claiming to be tech support who wants remote access to your device.
- A fake delivery message with a link to "reschedule".
- A romance or friendship scam that moves quickly to requests for money.

## How do I defend against social engineering?
Pause when a message pushes urgency. Verify identity using a separate channel you already trust, such as a known phone number or in-person. Never share passwords or one-time codes with anyone: legitimate staff never need them. Don't let unknown people into secure areas or plug in unknown USB drives. Limit what you post publicly, since attackers use it to sound convincing. If something feels off, it's fine to say no and check first.

## What should I do if I think I've been tricked?
Don't be embarrassed; skilled attackers fool many people. Act quickly: change any password you shared, contact your bank or provider if money or card details were involved, and enable multi-factor authentication. Keep screenshots or messages as evidence, report the scam to your school IT contact and to the platform, and file an incident report. Fast reporting often limits the damage and protects other people.

## What if someone threatens to share my private photos or messages?
This kind of blackmail is common and it is never your fault. Stop replying, don't pay (payment usually leads to more demands), take screenshots of the messages and account details, block the account and report it to the platform. Tell a trusted adult, teacher or school safeguarding contact, and report it to the police if you are being threatened. Help is available, and acting early helps most.
